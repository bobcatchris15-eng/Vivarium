# Water layers and visible spring flow

The water-table layer is a hydrostatic surface for ponds, creeks, inlets and shoreline water. Its elevation comes from the configured water table; `WaterMesh.BuildWaterTable` clips it against the terrain and island. Ecological depth and wetness queries include that reservoir. It remains separate from mobile volume injected by springs.

The current production owner is `SurfaceLiquid` / `SurfaceFluidDynamics`. It conserves supported column volume and momentum and transfers unsupported liquid to three-dimensional ballistic parcels. `WaterSurfaceRenderer` uploads immutable depth, head, velocity and support snapshots to persistent subdivided GPU tiles; `BallisticFluidRenderer` presents unsupported sheets and droplets. Persistence and recharge accounting are integrated. The older statement that an unintegrated particle prototype was the current implementation is obsolete; earlier rejected depth-grid pictures are historical evidence, not acceptance of today's renderer.

The October 16 visual pass gives the fixed table and moving films a common canopy reflection and Beer-Lambert transmission model. The camera still sees a pure black background. Flow highlights advect using simulated velocity; refraction and reflection reveal a surface above the bed rather than relying on cyan bed tint. A short presentation-only transition settles the final film onto the table plane, then the reservoir presents that surface alone. This does not alter stored volume, table elevation, terrain geometry, save schema or simulation queries.

Validation and limits are recorded in `docs/references/forest-2026-10-15/WATER.md` and `build/forest-reference-20261015/water-impact/`. Focused water/drainage tests are numerical contracts; native matched images and corrected above-water clips are the visual evidence. They cover the authored creek, an early thin film and the shallow bayou receiver. They do not establish every possible edited shoreline, waterfall, camera or performance condition. Terrain classification remains soil-based; placed rock props retain their own substrate and material.
