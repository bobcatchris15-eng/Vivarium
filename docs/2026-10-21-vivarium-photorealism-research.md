# 2026-10-21 — Vivarium photorealism: rendering analysis and implementation catalogue

**Research and analysis only. No renderer changes, asset changes, builds, or new runtime benchmarks were performed for this report.**

The strongest opportunity is to make illumination, material response, geometry, and contact agree at ordinary viewing distances. Vivarium already has many sophisticated individual effects. The remaining synthetic appearance is unlikely to disappear through another global increase in transmission, normal strength, green saturation, or texture resolution.

My first priorities would be: audit the custom leaf light response; introduce spatially varying indirect illumination; improve the geometry and shading of overlapping leaves; replace conspicuous ground-layer transitions with structure-aware blending; and make water reflect and respond to its immediate surroundings. Establish a calibrated comparison fixture before choosing production settings.

This is a catalogue of options, not a recommendation to implement every item. The priorities below are my engineering judgments, not measured rankings.

**Scope and evidence**

Inspected the active E:/Vivarium checkout on main, whose HEAD at the start was 6e044a8bb552ea4e1b6c855fe6a3f8bb22cbd47a. The checkout contains substantial ongoing flora edits; this report describes files read during the review, not an immutable clean commit. The existing graph was used for navigation and checked against current source because it predates recent work.

The project targets Godot.NET.Sdk/4.7.1 and .NET 8, with Forward+ declared in project.godot. Saved visual evidence was inspected from the October 16 water work, October 18 foliage/moss work, and newer vaultfern and startup reviews. Those pictures have different dates and source states. They are useful observations, not matched comparisons of today's entire checkout. The startup screenshot explicitly says plant detail is still loading, so it is excluded from mature-world appearance judgments and performance conclusions.

Official engine documentation and original research were checked on October 21, 2026. Proposed API use still needs validation in the actual executable. As a limited local capability check, the installed Debug GodotSharp assembly contains the names AreaLight3D, CompositorEffect, ReflectionProbe, and CameraAttributesPhysical; this establishes binding presence, not tested rendering behavior.

**Specific findings that should shape the next experiments**

- **Direct leaf reflectance may be controlled by the wrong quantity.** The custom light processor derives f0 from SPECULAR_AMOUNT. Godot documents that value as a property of the light, with 1.0 for directional lights; it is not the leaf's fragment SPECULAR. Under the current directional key/fill, the written formula therefore selects the same f0 endpoint. The visual effect remains unmeasured, but the code is sufficient reason to audit material control before tuning it further. [Godot spatial shader light inputs](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/shaders/shader_reference/spatial_shader.html).
- **The canopy is directional but not locally occluded.** A global radiance field plus scalar/local-height AO cannot describe the distinct illumination of two overlapping leaves at different positions. This is a more promising gap than another pigment-noise layer.
- **There is no native GI/probe setup in the searched game source.** Searches did not find SDFGI, VoxelGI, or ReflectionProbe construction/configuration in the inspected game C#/scene paths. SSIL is present at high quality, but does not replace a spatial GI solution. This is a source observation, not a runtime scene-tree inspection.
- **Some existing filters can conceal material differences.** The leaf shader already increases roughness from normal derivatives and clamps the result to at least 0.35. Determine whether this is suppressing wanted waxy-leaf distinction before adding more specular gain.
- **Water bypasses ordinary material reflections.** The standing and dynamic surface shaders are unshaded composites. Their reflection helper directly evaluates the analytic canopy. Adding a native ReflectionProbe or turning on SSR does not automatically replace that helper.
- **Water roughness is not uniformly implemented.** mobile_roughness is consumed by the airborne water shaders, but the shared analytic reflection helper does not use it to filter standing/mobile-surface reflection. This is a concrete optical inconsistency to investigate, not a claim that all water roughness is unused.
- **A previous reflection experiment failed for known reasons.** The preserved [rejection receipt](../build/forest-reference-20261015/local-water-reflection-20261016/rejected.json) reports broken SSR silhouettes, then ghosty mottled fallback transitions. Any new local-reflection proposal must address those failure modes explicitly.
- **Past optical gains were modest.** The [October 18 foliage report](visual-lab/foliage-optics-2026-10-18.md) and [scattering exploration](visual-lab/foliage-scattering-exploration-2026-10-18.md) distinguish working mechanisms from satisfactory realism. I agree with that distinction after inspecting their saved images.
- **Normal-distance form still matters.** The inspected habitat frames show broad similarly shaded leaf groups, sharp ground-layer changes, and fine detail that can read as a flat printed pattern. The newer close vaultfern image has substantially more form detail, but also illustrates why repeated smooth material response and shading at overlaps deserve attention. These are visual judgments about the saved frames, not measured defects in every current species.

**Methods 01–10: illumination, exposure, and shadows**

**M01 — Build a small calibrated photographic comparison fixture. P0 / S–M.**  
Use the existing MaterialPortReview and world-review infrastructure to show a real production leaf cluster, moss, bark, soil, wet rock, shallow water, and one guardian patch under the same light. Add neutral diffuse and glossy reference objects; record exposure, camera, light phase, source state, readiness, and material overrides. Include a normal habitat view as well as a close-up. Compare one effect at zero, moderate, exaggerated, then selected strength. The exaggerated setting proves the mechanism reaches the image; it is not a production target. This follows the reference-validation approach described by [Lagarde and de Rousiers](https://seblagarde.wordpress.com/2015/07/14/siggraph-2014-moving-frostbite-to-physically-based-rendering/).

**M02 — Repair the separation between material reflectance and light intensity in the custom leaf BRDF. P0 / S–M.**  
In leaf_direct_light.gdshaderinc, introduce an explicit leaf/material dielectric reflectance parameter or a material-derived value passed from fragment processing. Keep light intensity/specular weighting separate. Audit Lambert, GGX, transmission, and the engine's PI convention against an equivalent StandardMaterial3D control. Also test whether the enabled transmission paths overlap; do not assume double counting without measuring it. Keep pigment and roughness fixed while changing only the material reflectance. A successful test should change the leaf's highlight response predictably without lifting diffuse shadows. This is the most concrete overlooked code issue found in this review.

**M03 — Make exposure depend on a photographic intent, and test whether the black void biases metering. P0 / S–M.**  
EnvironmentRig combines auto exposure, exposure scaling, AgX, contrast, and changing light energy. Begin with fixed exposure and a neutral target; then restore adaptation and compare identical habitats framed with different proportions of black. If the organism's apparent brightness changes materially with framing, investigate a subject/scene-weighted luminance meter or slower bounded adaptation. A custom meter is a proposed feature, not an assumed built-in option. Preserve exact black outside the habitat. Judge leaf/soil luminance and highlight clipping before changing saturation. Auto-exposure parameters and actual bound camera attributes should be included in every capture receipt.

**M04 — Replace the area-like unshadowed fill approximation with an actual finite light experiment. P1 / M.**  
The current fill is a DirectionalLight3D described as soft area-like lighting. Trial one broad overhead rectangular AreaLight3D to represent a grow panel or canopy opening, preserving total illumination as closely as possible. The current official class supports rectangular emission and soft shadows, but warns of additional Forward+ cost on visible objects. The custom leaf light processor also needs an area-light-aware branch; adding the node alone is not a complete experiment. Compare broad moving highlights, penumbra width, and canopy depth. [Godot AreaLight3D](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_arealight3d.html), [original LTC area-light research](https://eheitzresearch.wordpress.com/415-2/).

**M05 — Compute directional sky visibility and bent normals per plant or local region. P0 / M–L.**  
For each plant variant, bake a small directional visibility representation from its actual geometry: for example six directional factors or low-order spherical harmonics plus a bent normal. Combine it with a coarser neighborhood visibility field for nearby plants, logs, and rocks. Evaluate the hidden canopy illumination through those directions instead of applying only a height-based AO scalar. Bake in normalized plant space, rotate with the instance, and update neighborhood information when geometry changes. Start with a single overlapping leaf cluster. The goal is light from visible gaps and darkening from real obstruction, without multiplying the entire plant toward black.

**M06 — Add a small, presentation-only RGB irradiance volume. P1 / L.**  
Vivarium's bounded habitat makes a modest probe lattice plausible. As a starting experiment, use roughly 0.5–1 m spacing over the occupied habitat with extra vertical samples near the understory; choose actual density from error and cost. Store low-order directional RGB irradiance and visibility, update a limited number of probes per frame, and invalidate affected regions through RenderDirtyRegions. Sample the field for leaves, bark, ground, and guardians. Do not feed this display lighting into ecological light/resource simulation. Godot exposes environment irradiance overrides that may provide an integration route, but blend conventions and all affected materials need validation. Visibility-aware interpolation is essential to prevent glowing root cavities.

**M07 — Compare native GI approaches on a bounded patch before choosing one. P1 / M–L.**  
Try VoxelGI and SDFGI on the same small habitat fixture with explicit occluder classifications. SDFGI accepts dynamic lighting but does not support dynamic occluders/emissive surfaces as full contributors, making continuously growing vegetation a poor fit without a deliberate refresh/proxy strategy. VoxelGI is more appropriate to investigate for bounded dynamic contributors, though baking, voxel resolution, thin leaves, and sculpted terrain remain difficulties. Baked lightmaps suit static presentation pieces better than an evolving whole habitat. Use a thick-trunk/rock proxy plus leaf visibility treatment if that gives a better quality/cost balance. [Godot SDFGI limitations](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/global_illumination/using_sdfgi.html), [GI mode guidance](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/global_illumination/introduction_to_global_illumination.html).

**M08 — Add local reflection information to opaque wet and glossy surfaces. P1 / M.**  
Trial a few ReflectionProbe volumes around the wet bank, forest floor, and major canopy cluster. Refresh them on meaningful changes, not all every frame; keep update cost bounded. They could give wet bark, leaves, eyes, and rock a reflection that includes the habitat instead of only the synthetic canopy. Test parallax correction carefully: a nonrectangular habitat is not a box room. This is distinct from custom unshaded water, which needs M24/M26. Probe ambient contribution is also worth comparing as a cheaper approximation, with no assumption that it equals multi-bounce GI. [Godot reflection probes](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/global_illumination/reflection_probes.html).

**M09 — Represent foliage shadows as optical thickness, including colored transmission. Lab / L–XL.**  
Ordinary opaque shadowing cannot express light progressively filtered through several leaves. Build a low-resolution light-space leaf-density or optical-depth volume, accumulate wavelength/RGB-dependent extinction, and evaluate exp(-optical_depth) for the direct transmitted component. Keep woody occlusion opaque. Start with two leaves and a neutral receiver, then a shoot. This requires owning the relevant shadow contribution: simply multiplying an extra colored mask over already opaque leaf shadows can double-darken the scene. A coarse density field may be cheaper and more stable than resolving every lamina. Success means plausible layered shadows and filtered sunflecks, not universally green shade.

**M10 — Separate microcontact occlusion from broad canopy occlusion. P1 / M.**  
Reserve SSAO or a small screen-space contact pass for millimeter/centimeter attachment cues, while M05/M06 handle larger-scale illumination. Audit directional bias, normal bias, atlas resolution, and split allocation at actual leaf/root scale. A single large SSAO radius cannot both ground a tiny stem and shade a canopy correctly. Use close root/soil and stacked-leaf fixtures to find where contact detaches or becomes a dark halo. Account for AO already present in terrain materials and scanned data. The desired image has attached objects and readable cavities without dirt-colored outlines.

**Methods 11–23: material response, geometry, and surface continuity**

**M11 — Capture or acquire calibrated material sets with separate reflectance and thickness evidence. P1 / M.**  
For a few representative real materials, use cross-polarized illumination to isolate diffuse color, unpolarized/specular observations for the cuticle, and transmitted illumination for relative leaf thickness. Include a color/scale reference and front/back views. Fit normal/roughness from multiple lighting directions instead of deriving every channel from brightness. Keep albedo in the intended color space and normal/roughness/thickness as linear data. Apply this first to one glossy leaf, one matte leaf, moss, and damp humus. Existing texture pools and bindings are good insertion points; the improvement is coherent calibrated channels, not merely larger source images.

**M12 — Use an offline leaf reflectance/transmittance lookup table. Lab / M–L.**  
Replace independent artist gains with coupled reflectance and transmission responses parameterized by tissue thickness, pigment, water content, and dry matter. PROSPECT-family models are useful references for directional-hemispherical leaf spectra; they do not by themselves supply a complete angular cuticle BRDF. Integrate sampled spectra to the renderer's RGB illuminants offline and store a compact lookup table. Use that for tissue response while retaining a separately fitted surface lobe. Constrain reflected plus transmitted energy in a common convention. This is a better route to differentiated young, old, succulent, and senescent leaves than arbitrary extra green. [Leaf radiative-transfer models](https://www.artmotoolbox.com/index.php/rtms/rtms-leaf).

**M13 — Spend geometry on leaf shape at the frequencies people actually see. P0 / M.**  
Use LeafBlade, PinnateFrond, and species builders to express midrib curvature, cupping, twist, margin roll, petiole transitions, and thickness where they change silhouette or cast visible shadows. Existing shader normal curvature cannot move a silhouette or create self-occlusion. Distribute curvature asymmetrically and by developmental cohort, preserving stable organ IDs across detail tiers. Avoid adding uniform subdivisions to every leaf. Test ordinary habitat framing, a grazing highlight sweep, and backlighting; if the effect is visible only in an extreme macro crop, its global geometry cost is difficult to justify.

**M14 — Give material families genuinely different lobes and anatomical masks. P1 / M.**  
Treat glossy upper cuticle, rough underside, wax bloom, reed epidermis, fungal cap, gill, bark, lichen, and moist animal skin as different optical families. Current controls already distinguish some of them; use the common fixture to verify the distinctions survive actual shading and distance. Add region masks for wax accumulation, worn edges, veins, joints, and wetness rather than multiplying whole species by a stronger sheen. Audit the shared custom light path's behavior on non-leaf surfaces and preserve the membrane mask. A waxy leaf should be recognizable under neutral lighting without relying on hue.

**M15 — Allocate a frequency budget to normals and roughness. P1 / M.**  
The leaf shader already filters normal variance into roughness. Extend that idea coherently across material families and inspect whether multiple normal layers or a roughness floor overflatten the result. Treat broad curvature as geometry/low-frequency normals, venation as mid-frequency structure, and unresolved wax or pores as a statistical lobe. Bake variance-aware mip information where useful; do not treat averaging normals as equivalent to averaging their shading. Test camera retreat and lateral motion, looking for stable highlight energy rather than glitter or disappearing gloss. [Normal-map filtering research](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/cg/normalmap/index.html).

**M16 — Audit normal coordinate frames, especially triplanar transitions and deformed surfaces. P1 / S–M.**  
Terrain mixes samples from multiple projection planes. Verify that each sampled normal is rotated into the same world/tangent frame, with correct axis sign and handedness, before blending. The same audit applies to leaf derivative frames, nonuniform scaling, backfaces, and animated normals. A texture can look detailed while its highlights imply the wrong shape. Use a deliberately obvious directional bump pattern and rotate a single light/camera around each projection seam. Treat this as an audit hypothesis, not a confirmed global normal bug. Preserve Godot's clockwise front-face convention established by prior mesh reviews.

**M17 — Replace recognizable texture repetition with correlated stochastic tiling. P1 / M.**  
Add hex/triangle patch sampling to soil, bark, and rock where repeated scan features are visible. Apply the same patch transforms and blend weights to albedo, roughness, height, and normal data; rotate normals consistently. Histogram-preserving techniques can maintain contrast that simple randomized blending loses, while practical hex tiling offers a more direct integration route. Compare oblique ground views and exposed trunk sides; inspect seams and mip transitions. Avoid randomizing anatomical bark direction or vein topology. This extends existing UV distortion rather than assuming tiling is currently untreated. [Deliot and Heitz](https://eheitzresearch.wordpress.com/738-2/), [Practical Real-Time Hex-Tiling](https://jcgt.org/published/0011/03/05/).

**M18 — Blend terrain by structure and frequency, not just color weights. P0 / M.**  
Current substrate, moisture, litter, and coverage fields already provide meaningful ownership. Keep those masks, but blend low-frequency color over broad transitions while selecting high-frequency stones, leaf fragments, and pore features over narrower height/structure-aware transitions. Add genuine height metadata where available; albedo darkness is not automatically depth. GPU-friendly Laplacian blending is an interesting alternative to ordinary crossfades because it aims to preserve detail through broad material transitions. Test dry-to-wet, soil-to-gravel, and sparse-litter edges. No moss or litter should appear where its governing coverage/mass policy forbids it. [GPU-Friendly Laplacian Texture Blending](https://jcgt.org/published/0014/01/02/paper.pdf).

**M19 — Add selective mid-scale soil and litter geometry. P0 / M.**  
The missing scale is often clods, curled litter edges, small roots, stone facets, and gaps beneath debris—not microscopic pores. Expand SoilDetailRenderer and existing ground-detail placement with a bounded nearby population tied to stored litter/substrate, concentrating geometry where it changes grazing silhouettes and contact shadows. Reuse instances and preserve terrain support. At distance, transfer the visual coverage into the terrain material without changing its mean color. Avoid dense uniformly distributed gravel and identical upright flakes. Compare a low camera and a normal overview; detail should establish scale without becoming sparkling clutter.

**M20 — Model wetness as porous absorption plus a separate surface film. P1 / M.**  
Terrain already darkens and changes roughness with moisture, and prior work correctly removed a silver second mirror under water. Extend that with distinct pore saturation and exposed water-film coverage. Saturated soil can darken while remaining rough; a thin film on a rock can add a smooth outer lobe over a rough substrate. Let concavity, slope, material porosity, and actual wet support control coverage. A two-layer approximation should attenuate the underlying contribution instead of simply adding unlimited gloss. Test dry/damp/wet states under one light and exposure. [Lagarde's wet-surface analysis](https://seblagarde.wordpress.com/2013/03/19/water-drop-3a-physically-based-wet-surfaces/).

**M21 — Treat every major contact boundary as a small three-dimensional transition. P1 / M.**  
Use the existing contact mask and geometry support to create root collars, compressed humus, partly embedded stones, leaf litter caught against logs, and small soil displacement around stems. Let moisture/pigment/roughness changes agree with these shapes. Projected decals alone are useful for color, but cannot bridge a visible gap beneath a root or rock. Concentrate effort on contacts exposed to the player, using nearby geometry and simpler distant shading. Maintain the current authoritative distinction between ecological occupancy and anonymous presentation cover. Judge in silhouette and side light, not only from above.

**M22 — Render moss as a porous canopy rather than a smooth green solid. P1 / M–L.**  
Retain the recent colony-shaped meshes and occupancy constraints. Add a near-field representation with sparse species-specific shoot tips, while the underlying material models shadowed gaps, broad multiple scattering, and selective glints at tips. Use the existing aggregate helper as the experiment seam. Match average reflectance and silhouette across distances; indiscriminate tiny triangles previously produced bright speckles. Keep cushion, mat, sphagnum-like, and lichen forms distinct. A useful test has front light, grazing light, and a camera retreat showing the transition from visible shoots to an aggregate. M39/M40 are more ambitious versions of this idea.

**M23 — Give exposed cutaway surfaces geological structure and scale. P2 / M.**  
The diorama cutaway is part of the final image, so uniform layered bands or stretched bark/soil can undermine otherwise good foliage. Review strata.gdshader and PilotTreeRenderer for meter-consistent texture scale, sediment lens shapes, embedded stones/roots, fracture direction, and nonuniform transition depth. Preserve the intentional clean cut plane while allowing the material within it to describe actual structure. This is especially useful at overview distance where the habitat boundary occupies many pixels. Avoid spending all geometric detail on a surface that remains hidden during normal use. Compare the slice and living surface under identical exposure.

**Methods 24–29: water that belongs to the habitat**

**M24 — Retry local water reflection only with a different confidence and fallback design. P1 / L.**  
The old SSR attempt exposed bank/stem shapes but broke into noisy silhouettes and mottled transitions. A new design should return both reflected color and hit confidence, reject foreground/wrong-depth hits, and use a stable local cubemap or analytic canopy fallback. Test a limited planar reflection camera for nearly flat standing ponds; exclude the water itself and keep the reflection camera invisible to the user. Sloping films need a different approximation. Feed the result into mobile_reflection or a replacement shared optical interface; ordinary engine SSR alone will not enter the current unshaded composite. Reject the experiment if moving-camera ghosts remain.

**M25 — Replace screen-UV refraction offsets with a bounded refracted-ray thickness test. P1 / L.**  
water_stream.gdshader currently uses screen-depth differences and a depthf-based cap for optical path length. Prototype Snell refraction through the surface normal and trace against available scene depth or a local heightfield, with strict invalid-hit fallback. Use the resulting path length for absorption, so shallow edges stay clear and deeper water gains color for a defensible reason. Handle foreground occluders, screen edges, submerged cameras, and thin films explicitly. The screen buffer cannot reveal hidden geometry; a heightfield or secondary scene representation may be needed for stable important views. Preserve the existing hydrology and water geometry.

**M26 — Give all water layers the same roughness-aware reflection and light response. P1 / M–L.**  
The standing and surface-film paths currently evaluate a single direction in the analytic canopy; their shared helper does not broaden the reflection using mobile_roughness. Implement a prefiltered environment lookup or a cheap stable directional convolution, driven by unresolved ripple slope variance. Keep geometry normals and reflection filtering separate so rough water does not merely become distorted sharp noise. Include the actual light's finite highlight consistently rather than relying only on an approximate bright canopy opening. Match table water, films, sheets, and droplets at their joins. Avoid lighting a precomposed transmitted/reflected color a second time.

**M27 — Add contact menisci and persistent wet margins at real interfaces. P2 / M–L.**  
A narrow rising waterline against a stem, rock, or bank communicates surface tension and scale. Generate a small contact-distance field from water support and solid geometry, then add a bounded meniscus normal/geometry treatment close to those interfaces. The existing cut-face meniscus is not a general object-contact meniscus. Derive wet margins from actual film/contact state; if drying history is desired, define a deliberate presentation cache with invalidation and save/load policy rather than inventing biological history. Test shallow puddles and changing water levels. The effect must not form a bright outline around every object.

**M28 — Derive caustic structure from the same water surface that refracts the view. Lab / L.**  
Terrain already has a restrained procedural bed pattern, kept out of emission. A more coherent version would project refracted light through the water normal field onto actual submerged receivers and accumulate a filtered focusing estimate. Modulate by incoming light visibility, water absorption, and receiver normal. Start with one shallow pool under one light; cloudy lighting should suppress high-contrast caustics. This is a secondary refinement after M24–M26. If the light, ripple normal, and caustic animation disagree, the result can look less physical despite being more detailed.

**M29 — Add sparse secondary water events driven by existing motion and contact. P2 / M–L.**  
Use current flow, falling-water impacts, and GuardianWaterContact to drive small capillary ripples, bubbles, drips, and occasional foam where justified. Derive event intensity from actual impact or flow changes instead of spawning decorative white particles everywhere. Advect ripple/foam history through a small render-only field with a bounded lifetime and clear pause behavior. The existing primary fluid simulation and interpolation should remain authoritative. Test a single animal footfall or stream impact, then the whole habitat. These cues can make even an approximate optical surface feel physically connected.

**Methods 30–37: temporal appearance, creatures, atmosphere, and camera**

**M30 — Evaluate image reconstruction as part of realism. P1 / S–M.**  
Compare the current default FXAA, 4x MSAA, native-resolution TAA, and—if affordable—native-resolution FSR2 on identical camera paths. MSAA helps mesh edges but does not solve shader/specular aliasing; temporal methods can reduce that noise while introducing blur or ghosts. Existing normal filtering remains useful under every choice. Inspect leaves, fine stems, moss, moving animals, and water, including previous-frame deformation handling. For future alpha-card experiments, test alpha-to-coverage separately; the existing geometric leaves do not require conversion to cards. Do not choose an AA method from a stationary beauty frame. [Godot antialiasing guidance](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/3d_antialiasing.html).

**M31 — Make detail transitions preserve optical coverage, not only triangle budget. P1 / M–L.**  
Projected-size tiers already exist. Audit whether their transition preserves silhouette area, visible leaf-normal distribution, shadow density, material average, and aggregate roughness. A plant can keep its bounding box while becoming perceptually much thinner or brighter. Use stable organ identities, carefully chosen crossfades or geometric morphs, and a distant representation matched to the near plant's integrated appearance. Prefer spending polygons on an outline or leaf overlap over hidden interior tessellation. Verify with a slow camera retreat and frozen world so changes caused by detail policy are visible.

**M32 — Add only motivated, coherent motion; keep the current no-wind choice explicit. P2 / M–L.**  
The inspected production path disables ambient plant sway. If movement is desired later, distinguish a still enclosure from an outdoor wind scene. Use a shared low-frequency flow field, branch stiffness, delayed leaf response, and shielding by neighboring objects; do not assign independent sine motion to every blade. An enclosure may benefit more from animal contact, droplets, small settling motions, and slow growth than perpetual wind. Reuse attachment metadata and rotate normals consistently. [Wētā's plant-animation work](https://www.wetafx.co.nz/assets/Uploads/PDFs/plantsPaper.pdf) is useful inspiration for hierarchical response, not a ready-made real-time solution.

**M33 — Smooth lighting/cache updates without changing simulation time. P1 / M.**  
EnvironmentRig changes the canopy radiance inputs on a four-second phase while direct lighting follows the clock smoothly. Check whether specular highlights or ambient balance step at those boundaries; do not assume the step is visible without a capture. If it is, interpolate between cached lighting states or distribute updates across frames. Apply the same principle to new irradiance/probe fields and texture updates. Keep exposure adaptation from chasing these updates. Use the existing presentation-only clock and render snapshots, preserving simulation pause/speed semantics and deterministic state.

**M34 — Improve creature realism through material-specific detail and actual support. P1 / M–L.**  
Use GuardianRenderer's existing per-material treatment as the starting point: separate wet nose/eye, rough skin, scales, keratin, and short-fur response. For the coati, preserve the user's established direction of corrected anatomy plus directional fur normals; do not restore the rejected dense fiber coat. A restrained anisotropic/fiber lobe can complement those normals. Then inspect skinned foot support, muzzle contact, body compression, and plant/water reaction in motion. A high-quality roughness map will not compensate for hovering or sliding. Extend analogous contact and material checks to small fauna where their projected size makes it worthwhile.

**M35 — Make bioluminescence affect nearby surfaces at a controlled scale. P2 / M.**  
NightFloraMaterials and leaf emission already exist, while high-tier SSIL only provides a screen-dependent portion of nearby light. Trial a limited number of local proxy lights or injection into M06's irradiance field, grouped by emission clusters. Match emitted appearance, local illumination, and exposure; suppress any broad glow that lifts the required black void. Test a glowing organ beside a neutral leaf and soil patch, then move it off screen. The neighborhood should not lose all illumination simply because the source is no longer visible. Avoid one dynamic light per tiny emitting organ.

**M36 — Tie bounded mist to habitat structure and illumination. P2 / M.**  
EnvironmentRig already supplies a bounded fog volume at high quality. Explore a low-resolution density field shaped by actual wet areas, cool depressions, and canopy shelter, without claiming these render fields are a new weather simulation. Localize volume scattering to real light paths and keep near objects readable. Test whether a small amount of participating media separates overlapping layers better than more global contrast. Respect the exact-black camera background and clear habitat boundary; a full-screen haze overlay is a poor fit. Volumetric cost and temporal streaking must be judged in moving camera views.

**M37 — Use camera optics selectively, primarily for inspection/photo mode. P2 / S–M.**  
Match field of view, camera height, working distance, and exposure to the visual reference before adding lens effects. Trial moderate physical depth of field only when the subject and scale justify it; a permanently blurred habitat would interfere with inspection. Keep sharpening restrained and avoid chromatic aberration or film grain as a substitute for material detail. A photo mode can accumulate samples or render above display resolution without setting the gameplay cost. CameraAttributesPhysical is present in the installed binding, but an actual optical workflow still needs calibration and a legibility review.

**Methods 38–44: unconventional and experimental directions**

**M38 — Build an offline path-traced reference for one exact habitat patch. P1 / M–L.**  
Export a frozen production mesh patch, camera, material intent, and lights to a path tracer such as Cycles or PBRT. Translate shaders deliberately; raw glTF export will not reproduce custom Godot leaf/water models. Render neutral diffuse, specular, transmission, and indirect components separately. Use the result to answer which approximation matters: missing inter-leaf bounce, incorrect roughness, insufficient geometry, or water transport. The offline render is a diagnostic target, not evidence of game performance. A static patch is enough to guide several months of renderer decisions without replacing the engine.

**M39 — Use a compact measured or baked bidirectional texture function for difficult aggregates. Lab / L.**  
For moss, fuzzy bark, fungal gills, or rough litter, ordinary albedo/normal/roughness maps cannot fully describe view-dependent self-shadowing and parallax. Capture or render the material patch under multiple light/view directions, then compress those responses into a small basis or texture array. Apply it to a near-field patch with a conventional fallback. Keep directional sampling, memory use, interpolation, and seams visible in the experiment. This is more plausible as a specialized material than as a universal surface system. [Original BTF acquisition/rendering survey](https://cg.cs.uni-bonn.de/backend/v2/files/publications/mueller-2005-acquisition/pdf/eg_star_2004_2bb41d3a56.pdf).

**M40 — Replace unresolved shoot geometry with a shallow microvolume. Lab / L–XL.**  
Bake moss or a dense leaf cluster into density, orientation, and reflectance fields; render a bounded shallow volume or prefiltered representation when individual elements become subpixel. SGGX microflakes offer a principled model for anisotropic aggregate scattering and filtering across scales. This could preserve the soft, porous appearance that smooth cushion meshes lose. Start with one moss patch, and compare against actual shoot geometry for silhouettes, highlights, and occlusion. A volume can also look like fog or a solid sponge, so retain a surface/geometry fallback. [The SGGX Microflake Distribution](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2015-08_The-SGGX-microflake/sggx.pdf).

**M41 — Precompute per-plant light transfer instead of solving every internal bounce at runtime. Lab / L.**  
The deterministic species/variant builders make repeatable transfer data possible. For each geometry tier and growth state, bake a low-rank mapping from incident light directions to outgoing diffuse/transmitted response, or at least self-visibility plus local bounce. Rotate it with the plant and combine it with the neighborhood field from M06. Ordinary AO stores only obstruction; this stores some directional transport and color bleeding. Growth changes and deformation limit reuse, so begin with stable mature specimens. Keep the data under a geometry/material hash so a rebuilt species cannot silently use an incompatible bake.

**M42 — Investigate neural appearance or neural asset transport as an offline-to-runtime compression route. Lab / XL.**  
Two concrete research directions are [Real-Time Neural Appearance Models](https://research.nvidia.com/publication/2023-05_real-time-neural-appearance-models), which compress complex material response, and [8DNA: 8D Neural Asset Light Transport](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/rtr/publication/wu20268dna/), published in 2026, which learns precomputed asset transport including near-field illumination. A bounded test could train one moss/leaf-cluster asset from reference renders and compare a tiny decoder with a conventional lookup table. Neither is a drop-in Godot feature, and their reported results do not establish feasibility on this project's hardware. Geometry changes, training cost, runtime support, and identity preservation are major constraints.

**M43 — Consider a separate high-end traced preview/photo renderer, with DDGI as a smaller research step. Lab / XL.**  
A ray-traced irradiance cache is a more bounded ambition than replacing the whole renderer with path tracing. [DDGI irradiance fields](https://www.jcgt.org/published/0008/02/01/paper-lowres.pdf) provide a reference for visibility-aware probes; [ReSTIR GI](https://research.nvidia.com/publication/2021-06_restir-gi-path-resampling-real-time-path-tracing) and [neural radiance caching](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/rtr/publication/muller2021nrc/) address more ambitious transport. These require ray access, acceleration structures, history rejection, and reconstruction integration. A conventional material shader or an empty CompositorEffect cannot provide all of that automatically. Keep this as a distinct renderer investigation with an explicit hardware target, not a prerequisite for near-term realism gains.

**M44 — Fit a small set of physical controls against multiple references, then rank errors by scale. Lab / M–L.**  
Use the fixed fixture and optional offline reference to optimize a limited parameter set: reflectance, roughness, transmission/thickness, light size, exposure, and coarse curvature. Start with deterministic parameter sweeps or derivative-free optimization; a neural optimizer is unnecessary. Weight silhouette/contact, medium-scale shading, and highlight shape separately rather than minimizing whole-screen RGB error dominated by the black background. Hold out a different camera/light setup to catch overfitting. Match reference crop scale and illumination, since the supplied forest photographs are not radiometrically calibrated. The result should be interpretable material settings, not an image filter that invents geometry.

**A practical sequence of bounded experiments**

| Experiment | Change under test | Keep fixed | Evidence that would justify further work |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A. Leaf response | M02, followed by measured top/back roughness and reflectance | Mesh, pigment, light, exposure | Material controls produce distinct plausible lobes in direct-only and environment-only views |
| B. Overlapping foliage | M05 on one production shoot, then a neighbor | Leaf material, geometry, camera | More convincing depth and spatial illumination without uniformly darkening the plant |
| C. Ground continuity | M18 plus a separate M19 geometry trial | Substrate/coverage/litter state | Fewer painted boundaries and better depth in ordinary ground views |
| D. Finite illumination | M04 with the custom leaf path handled | Incident-light reference target and exposure | Better penumbrae/highlight shapes; tolerable measured GPU cost |
| E. Water locality | M24 confidence/fallback first, then M25/M26 | Saved water/terrain state, camera path, fluid phase | Recognizable stable nearby reflections, correct shallow depth, no ghosts or layer seams |
| F. Temporal stability | M30/M31 after a material candidate is chosen | World and light sequence | Fine detail survives movement without excessive blur, flicker, or detail-tier pumping |
| G. Reference oracle | M38 on the same patch | Geometry and intended physical material values | Clear attribution of the largest remaining error to transport, geometry, or material response |

These experiments are proposals. None were run as part of this analysis.

**What I would avoid prioritizing**

- Another blanket transmission/green-brightness increase. Earlier stress tests already demonstrated neon and waxy failure modes.
- More microscopic texture noise before fixing medium-scale structure and lighting.
- Stronger global AO as a substitute for spatial indirect illumination.
- A large HDR sky behind the habitat, since the current black backdrop is deliberate.
- Assuming a native GI, reflection, or area-light toggle automatically integrates with every custom material.
- Repeating the rejected SSR blend with only different gain values.
- Reintroducing dense coati fur geometry against the established art direction.
- Whole-renderer replacement before comparing a calibrated patch.
- Generative image enhancement as the primary gameplay renderer: stable anatomy, contact, readable simulation state, and consistency across views need to remain under project control.
- Using average FPS, a successful shader compile, or a visually busy close-up as proof that photorealism improved.

**Acceptance and measurement**

Judge a proposed change in a close view, the ordinary interaction view, an overview, and a short moving-camera sequence. Include backlighting, shade, wet/dry surfaces, day/night where relevant, and dense/mature content. Match the saved world, camera, exposure policy, light phase, CPU animation state, shader time, and renderer readiness. Verify that presentation changes preserve authoritative simulation state.

For performance, use the intended device, resolution, quality tier, and a warmed repeatable scene. Record CPU/GPU frame time where available, tail latency, draw/triangle/overdraw pressure, and cache-update spikes. Do not infer a hard frame-rate floor from per-second averages. The older GTX 1080 receipts in the repository are historical context, not measurements of any method proposed here.

For image comparisons, mask the background and evaluate specific regions of interest. Whole-screen RGB mean differences can hide meaningful leaf changes or reward unrelated terrain/exposure changes. Human comparison against scale-appropriate references remains necessary; there is no trustworthy single photorealism score.
